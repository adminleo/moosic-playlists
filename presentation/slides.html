1 — the question

Moosic builds playlists by hand. It does not scale.

Spotify publishes nine numeric features per track. Can K-Means turn those into playlists?

The honest version has two halves, and they do not get the same answer: is there similarity signal in the features at all — and is partitioning the catalogue the right way to use it?

2 — yes, the signal is real

Nearest neighbours share an artist 19.5× more often than chance

A proxy anyone can check: two tracks by the same artist are usually more alike than two at random.

The seed already beats the cluster five times over. That is the first hint about what the product should do.

3 — but the clusters are about recording

Three measurements, one conclusion

No natural number of playlists. Silhouette falls from 0.309 at k=4 to 0.123 at k=40 with no peak. A continuum — k is a business decision wearing a lab coat.

The separating features are production. Quiet acoustic instrumentals (loudness −2.02σ, energy −1.85σ, acousticness +1.80σ). Energetic, undanceable, joyless (energy +0.91σ, danceability −1.14σ, valence −0.83σ). "Sounds like it was played live" spans every genre.

The same song, recorded twice, splits. 92 titles exist in two artists' recordings. Only 22 — 23.9 % — land in the same cluster.

5 — what to do instead

Seed and neighbours, not partitioning

Moosic's product is a playlist built around a mood. That is a retrieval problem, not a clustering one.

  • No choice of k to defend.
  • A curator steers by picking the seed.
  • 3.86 % against the cluster's 0.78 % on the same yardstick.

Clustering keeps a job — a coarse first pass, eight buckets a curator can work through. It is just not the thing that makes the playlist.

The features were good enough and the method was wrong, and no accuracy number anywhere would have told us — because this failure only shows up when you ask what the clusters are made of.
